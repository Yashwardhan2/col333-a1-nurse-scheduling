1. MODELLING THE PROBLEM

We modelled the roster as a CSP in the standard way: one variable per (nurse, day) pair, so N*D variables over the domain {M, A, E, R, B}. Writing the nine constraints out, the first thing we noticed was that they fall into two groups that never talk to each other. H2, H3, H5, H6, H8 and H9 only constrain a single nurse's row across time; H4 and H7 only constrain a single day's column.

That split shaped everything else, and gives a fact we lean on in Part B: if we swap two nurses' shifts on the same day, the day's multiset of shifts is unchanged, so H4 and H7 cannot break. Only the two rows move, at days d-1, d and d+1.

The column side simplifies further. Let b_d be the number of B shifts on day d. Day d then needs m-b_d nurses on M, a-b_d on A, e on E and b_d on B, consuming m+a+e-b_d distinct nurses. So H4 and H7, which look like awkward higher-order constraints, become "pick disjoint sets of known sizes" once b_d is chosen. We keep b_d as small as H7 allows, raising it only when a day cannot field enough distinct nurses, since a B costs two units of K and H6 forces R or E the next day.

2. PART A: FINDING A VALID ROSTER

Part A is graded on how fast a valid roster appears, so we layered the solver, cheap path first.

Before searching we check six necessary conditions and return the empty object if any fails -- the "detect inevitable failures early" idea, pushed past forward checking. Two counting arguments do most of the work. On any day after the first, the m nurses who worked a morning are barred by H2 and the e on evenings by H3, so today's m mornings must come from outside a set of m+e; we sharpen this per day by how much of that set leave absorbs. Second, H2 stops a nurse taking B on consecutive days, so within any W days one nurse covers at most ceil(W/2) of them. That catches what a whole-horizon count cannot: a lone surgical nurse facing two adjacent surgical days is stuck however large K is. We checked that no condition fires on an instance we can actually solve.

Tier 1 is a randomised greedy pass over days. Within a day we fill the shift types in MRV order, taking whichever has least slack between eligible nurses and slots needed, recomputed each time since filling one shrinks the others' pools. The nurse is chosen by LCV, and the useful step was working out what "leaves maximum flexibility" means here. M, B and E are exactly the shifts barring a morning tomorrow, and tomorrow always needs m legal mornings, so they go first to nurses who cannot work tomorrow anyway -- blocking those costs nothing. After that we prefer the least-spent nurse, keeping K budgets level, and the shortest run, deferring rests H5 will force. Ties break randomly, which is what makes restarting worthwhile. We also hold surgical nurses back from ordinary shifts when remaining B demand nearly exhausts their budget; otherwise the solver spends their capacity early and cannot staff a late surgical day.

Tier 2 runs only if restarts dead-end. It backtracks over days rather than cells, so depth D instead of N*D, branching on b_d and on a few randomised fillings. Forward checking on day d+1 prunes most dead ends a level early, verifying it still has enough workers, enough legal mornings, and enough surgical nurses for its B quota. We restart with a growing node budget, since one unlucky subtree can otherwise absorb the whole limit.

3. PART B: LOWERING THE COST

The objective simplifies pleasantly: 3(x^2+y^2+z^2) - (x+y+z)^2 is identically (x-y)^2 + (y-z)^2 + (z-x)^2. So a zero-cost nurse has x = y = z, and summing that over all nurses forces D*m = D*a = D*e -- cost 0 is only reachable when m = a = e.

The search is local search. We start from the Tier 1 constructor, biasing value ordering toward whichever of M/A/E a nurse has least of, then hill-climb over the same-day swap neighbourhood from section 1. Since those swaps cannot disturb H4 or H7, only row constraints need rechecking, and only near the swapped day; cost updates in O(1) over the two nurses. We accept equal-cost moves so the search crosses plateaus rather than stopping on the first shoulder.

To know when to stop we compute a lower bound and halt once the roster reaches it. The bound is an admissible heuristic obtained by relaxation: we drop the row constraints, keeping only H4's daily column sums and H8's budget. Every valid roster is still feasible for that relaxed problem, so its optimum cannot exceed the true one. Where it is small we solve it exactly, otherwise falling back on an analytic bound from Cauchy-Schwarz on the columns. This is only a stopping rule -- it never builds or repairs a roster, and removing it would change no output's validity.

It mattered more than we expected. On test3, K = 2 means no nurse can hold (1,1,1), so every working nurse costs at least 2 and the 18 units of work need at least 9 of them: 18 is unbeatable. The analytic bound alone says 0, so the search would have spent its budget chasing something unreachable.

We also let cost decide the b_d policy here. On a one-day horizon every working nurse costs exactly 2, even on B, so the cost is 2*(m+a+e-b) and more B is strictly better -- the opposite of what Part A wants. Neither policy dominates, so Part B builds under both and keeps the better.

4. RESULTS AND OBSERVATIONS

All three samples are solved and verified: Part A in roughly 0.05s each, Part B at a provably optimal cost (36, 24, 18) in under two seconds. Across 1034 further instances, Part A returns a valid roster on every satisfiable one and correctly reports the unsatisfiable ones in about 0.1s each.

Two things cost us the most time. Deadline handling: an early version treated a timeout as an ordinary branch failure, so unwinding the tree was itself exponential and a five-second limit ran over a minute. And diagnosis -- nearly every instance our search could not crack proved to be unsatisfiable, so the productive work was proving infeasibility, not strengthening the search. The clearest remaining limitation is that same-day swaps hold b_d fixed, so Part B cannot leave the b vector its construction chose.
